Surveillance Pricing: How Algorithms and Personal Data Can Change What You Pay

By Massoda · Updated September 2026 · 10 min read

Two people can visit the same website, look at the same product and receive different offers. The difference may not be demand, inventory or timing. It may be what an algorithm believes about them.

That possibility stopped being an abstract privacy debate when some readers of The Washington Post reportedly saw a blunt disclosure beside subscription offers: “This price was set by an algorithm using your personal data.” The wording came from a New York law requiring businesses to disclose personalized algorithmic pricing to consumers in the state.

The notice tells us something important, but not everything. It establishes that an algorithm and personal data were involved in setting the displayed price. It does not reveal which data were used, how heavily each signal was weighted, whether another person received a different offer or whether the goal was to estimate willingness to pay.

The larger technology story is the rise of what the US Federal Trade Commission calls surveillance pricing: systems that combine consumer data, experimentation and automated decision-making to tailor prices, discounts or product displays. These systems can make commerce more responsive. They can also make an ordinary purchase feel like an invisible negotiation in which only one side knows the rules.

Dynamic Pricing and Personalized Pricing Are Not the Same

Prices have always changed. Airlines adjust fares as seats fill. Hotels respond to seasons and events. Ride-hailing services respond to local demand. Retailers discount products as inventory ages.

That is dynamic pricing: a price changes because conditions change.

Personalized algorithmic pricing goes further. New York General Business Law Section 349-A defines it as dynamic pricing set by an algorithm using personal data linked directly or indirectly to a specific consumer or device. The law requires the notice to appear near the relevant price when the practice applies.

The distinction is simple but important:

  • Dynamic pricing: the concert ticket costs more because demand increased for everyone.
  • Segmented pricing: a student or loyalty member receives a defined discount.
  • Personalized pricing: the system uses data about a person or device to determine the price or offer shown to that individual.

Personalization can also operate without changing the sticker price. A system might choose which discount to display, rank more expensive products first, alter the length of a trial or decide who receives a retention offer. The commercial outcome changes even if the base price remains technically constant.

How the Technology Works

A personalized-pricing system is not one magical AI model. It is usually a pipeline.

1. Data collection

The system may receive first-party data such as account history, subscriptions, purchases, abandoned carts and engagement. It may also use device, location, referral, advertising or third-party data, subject to applicable law and company policy.

The FTC's 2025 market study found that pricing intermediaries can use information including location, demographics, browsing patterns, shopping history and even interactions such as mouse movements. The agency stressed that its public examples were aggregated or hypothetical because confidential business information was protected.

2. Identity and feature creation

Raw activity is converted into variables a model can use: purchase frequency, likelihood to cancel, price sensitivity, device category, recent searches or membership status. Identity systems may attempt to connect activity across sessions and devices.

3. Prediction and decisioning

A model estimates an outcome—perhaps whether a visitor will subscribe, abandon a cart or accept a particular offer. A rules engine or optimization service then selects a price, discount, ranking or promotion.

4. Experimentation

Businesses often run controlled tests to compare offers. Experimentation can improve products, but it becomes ethically sensitive when the system tests how much different people can be charged without explaining the basis of the difference.

5. Measurement and feedback

Purchases, cancellations and responses return to the system. Over time, the model learns which offers produce revenue or retention. If the objective rewards only short-term conversion, the system may optimize away trust without anyone explicitly programming it to do so.

What the Washington Post Example Proves—and What It Does Not

Reporting by Washingtonian documented the personalized-pricing disclosure appearing on some Washington Post subscription offers. The disclosure is meaningful because New York law specifies the wording businesses must use.

However, a careful analysis should avoid filling in missing details. The notice alone does not prove that the publication used sensitive traits, inferred a reader's income or deliberately charged the maximum that person could tolerate. It also does not tell us whether the personalized offer was higher or lower than a standard offer. New York's law contains an exception for certain offers to existing subscribers that are below their contracted subscription price.

That uncertainty is exactly why transparency must extend beyond a warning label. Consumers need enough information to understand the category of data used, the purpose of the decision and the choices available to them.

Why Businesses Find It Attractive

From a product team's perspective, personalization can solve real problems. A discount can retain someone who would otherwise cancel. A regional price can make a service affordable in a lower-income market. An introductory offer can reduce the risk of trying an unfamiliar product.

But the same infrastructure can be used to identify urgency, limited alternatives or a high willingness to pay. The technology does not carry an ethical position of its own; it executes the objective and constraints chosen by people.

A system optimized solely for immediate revenue may learn to extract more from customers who appear less likely to comparison-shop. A system optimized for long-term value may instead favor predictable pricing, useful discounts and lower churn. Product metrics define what the algorithm learns to value.

The Risks Engineers and Leaders Must Design Around

  • Opacity: customers cannot evaluate a price if they do not know why it differs.
  • Proxy discrimination: apparently neutral variables can correlate with income, race, age, disability or other protected and sensitive characteristics.
  • Security exposure: more consumer data creates a larger target and a greater consequence if systems are breached.
  • Feedback loops: past behavior can reinforce assumptions about what groups will buy or can afford.
  • Loss of trust: a legally compliant system can still feel unfair when customers discover that prices are individualized.

There is also a measurement problem. A short experiment may show higher revenue while missing the long-term costs of complaints, cancellations, regulatory attention and reputational harm.

What Responsible Personalized Pricing Looks Like

A responsible implementation begins before model training.

  1. Define a legitimate purpose. Write down why personalization is needed and which outcomes are unacceptable.
  2. Minimize data. Do not collect or retain a signal merely because it might improve prediction.
  3. Separate sensitive attributes. Test whether ordinary-looking variables act as proxies for protected characteristics.
  4. Provide a meaningful explanation. A disclosure should be visible, understandable and connected to a privacy choice—not hidden in legal text.
  5. Audit outcomes. Compare prices and discounts across groups, devices and locations to identify unjustified differences.
  6. Keep human accountability. A named team should own the system's behavior, complaints and rollback process.

For consumers, practical protections include comparing prices while signed out, reviewing tracking permissions, limiting unnecessary location access and checking subscription offers directly. These steps cannot rebalance the entire market, but they reduce some of the data available for profiling.

The Future of the Digital Price Tag

Online commerce is moving from “What does this product cost?” toward “What offer should this person see right now?” That shift makes pricing part of the software experience—and therefore part of privacy, security, UX and AI governance.

The most important question is not whether algorithms should ever influence prices. They already do. It is whether customers can understand the decision, whether organizations can defend it and whether the system creates value without exploiting informational power.

Personalization can make a product more relevant. Once it reaches the price tag, relevance is no longer enough. The system must also be worthy of trust.
