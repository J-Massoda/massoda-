Software Pilots

The Demo Worked. Would the Workday?

By Massoda · 11 October 2026

A prepared demo shows the clearest path through a product. A useful pilot lets the people who do the work interrupt it.

Imagine a service team reviewing a client-intake prototype. A fictional enquiry arrives, a colleague records the details and the screen shows the next stage. Then a staff member asks what happens when clients email half the documents a week late. Someone else asks who sees the request when the assigned colleague is away. A third says the team sometimes begins work with an approved exception.

The demonstration has not failed. Those questions reveal what must be tested before a real team could depend on the idea.

Choose one recognizable request

A pilot does not need to model an entire organization. Start with a task people can describe without a presentation: from new enquiry to ready for work, from appointment request to confirmed booking, or from tender receipt to a decision about whether to bid.

Ask the people doing the work where information arrives, who reads it, what is usually missing, who makes the next decision and how the recipient learns the result. Recreate the shape of that work using fictional names and documents. Do not ask a team to upload private customer records just to find out whether the interface makes sense.

Interrupt the happy path

Walk through the normal scenario once, then introduce one complication at a time. A detail is missing. The assigned person is unavailable. Two records might describe the same request. The customer changes an answer. A connected service times out. A task looks complete but someone is still waiting.

Observe the next action. Can the team see the last reliable state? Does the prototype expose uncertainty or hide it behind a green label? Can another colleague pick up the case? Is there a way to correct a mistake and explain why a decision changed?

These tests can also reveal that a process needs clearer ownership before software helps. That is a valuable pilot result.

Ask what would earn trust

“It looks good” is a promising reaction, but it does not establish fit. Ask a new colleague to explain the case from the screen. Ask a reviewer where an important detail came from. Ask what would happen if the organization returned to its existing workflow for a day. Ask whether users understand the difference between a suggestion and a confirmed action.

Collect feedback from more than the person who approves the purchase. Frontline staff often know the exceptions. A customer or recipient may care most about whether the next update is accurate. The same prototype can help one group and create extra work for another.

Define what the result can prove

A guided test with fictional data can show whether people understand the workflow and where the idea gets in their way. It cannot establish production security, privacy, accessibility, integration readiness or measured savings in a company that has not deployed it. Those require separate evaluation and scope.

At the end, write down what felt useful, what failed or confused people, and what would have to change. A decision not to continue is honest evidence. A decision to develop further should follow from what the team observed, not only the polish of the demo.
