How to Animate a Logo in Procreate Dreams: A Beginner-Friendly Process

By Massoda  •  Tutorial  •  2026

Logo animation looks intimidating from the outside — like it needs a full motion graphics suite and years of After Effects experience. It doesn't. Some of the cleanest logo animations I've built, including the ones for Fuse and Bpétale, were done almost entirely in Procreate Dreams, starting from a plain static mark.

Start With a Logo That Already Wants to Move

Before opening Procreate Dreams at all, look at the static logo itself. Does it have a curve, a swoosh, a letterform that implies direction? Fuse's wordmark has a soft, looping "s" that already reads as motion even sitting still — that's what made it a natural first animation project. If your logo is very rigid and geometric, that's fine too, but the animation approach will lean more toward a clean reveal than an organic trace.

Sketch the Motion Before You Animate It

The biggest mistake people make jumping into logo animation is opening the animation tool first. Sketch the motion on paper or in a few static frames first: what draws in first, what settles last, how long the whole thing should run. A logo animation that runs longer than 2-3 seconds usually overstays its welcome as a brand ident.

Building It in Procreate Dreams

Once the motion is planned, Procreate Dreams lets you build it frame by frame or with its animation timeline tools depending on the complexity. For something like a wordmark tracing itself in, I work close to the original letterforms — animating strokes to follow the mark's actual paths rather than adding unrelated flourishes. The goal is always that the animation should look like it belongs to the logo, not like an effect layered on top of it.

Keep the Loop Honest

If the animation is going to loop (for a website hero, a social post, a video intro), the first and last frame need to connect cleanly — no visible jump cut. I usually build in a beat of stillness at the end before the loop restarts, which reads as intentional rather than glitchy.

Export for Where It's Actually Going

A logo animation destined for a website loop should be exported as a lightweight MP4 or WebM, not a GIF — GIFs are far larger for the same visual quality and don't support the same colour depth. For social platforms, export at the aspect ratio the platform actually uses rather than cropping after the fact.

If you've got a static logo sitting unused because animating it feels like a bigger project than it is — it's usually a lot more approachable than it looks. I take on logo animation commissions if you'd rather hand it off entirely.
