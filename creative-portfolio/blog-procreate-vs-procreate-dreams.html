Procreate vs Procreate Dreams: Which Should You Use in 2026?

By Jean Emilien Massoda  •  Illustration & Motion  •  Updated 26 November 2026

Choose Procreate for drawing and painting. Choose Procreate Dreams when the main job is animation, timing, and combining moving elements. You can make short animations in Procreate, and you can draw directly in Dreams. The useful question is which kind of work you want to finish.

I work across illustration, visual identity, motion, and software. That makes the handoff between a finished image and a usable moving asset particularly interesting to me. A logo can look right on a canvas and still need considerable work before it functions as a short introduction on a website. This comparison follows that transition, using my Fuse and BPÉTALE projects to make the decisions concrete.

Updated 26 November 2026. The official help centre lists Procreate 5.4.15 and Procreate Dreams 2.1.3. Feature details below come from current official documentation. My portfolio projects illustrate creative decisions; they are not benchmarks or claims that I remade those projects in these releases.

Procreate vs Procreate Dreams: the practical comparison

Start with the deliverable. A portrait, packaging illustration, or detailed background involves decisions about shape, colour, texture, and composition. A moving identity involves those decisions plus duration, transitions, repetition, and the relationship between elements at different moments. These are connected disciplines, but the working surface you need changes when time becomes part of the brief.

The table is a starting point, rather than a universal ranking. Someone making a tiny drawn loop might finish entirely in Procreate. Someone who already has usable illustration assets might begin directly in Dreams. For a commission, I would choose the working method around the agreed outputs, the likely revisions, and the materials the client already owns.

What changed in the current generations?

Procreate 5.4: a more organised painting workspace

The official Procreate 5.4 overview describes brush libraries, 180 additional brushes across 18 sets, brush search, Files integration, and brush syncing through iCloud Drive. It also documents new Brush Studio controls and changes to the Colours panel, including Colour History on all supported iPad screen sizes. Existing custom brushes are available through the Classic Library after an upgrade.

For my kind of work, the useful question is whether a more organised set of tools helps me stay consistent across a project. Suppose I am drawing several supporting illustrations for one identity. I would rather return to a small, intentional brush selection than introduce a different texture each time I open a file. A larger library creates options; a project still benefits from restraint and a clear visual direction.

Dreams 2: comparisons based on the launch version need revisiting

The Dreams 2 feature overview documents a redesigned, multi-track Flipbook, Selection and Transform tools for drawings and Flipbooks, new animation brushes, keyboard customisation, GIF export, transparent video support, and broader Multi-select editing. Saying that Dreams cannot select part of a drawing or export a GIF would therefore misrepresent this generation.

That matters when deciding whether an older criticism still applies to your planned workflow. Before rejecting either app because of a review, check the version shown in that review and try the exact operation in current documentation. A feature being available does not automatically tell you how comfortable you will find it, but an outdated missing-feature list cannot settle the purchase decision.

Dreams 2.1: changes around the working experience

The Dreams 2.1 update notes add a movable Flipbook mini, navigation between drawings, Scrub Lock, adjustable Backstage visibility, clearer easing indicators, restoration of a previous Multi-select selection, and flip/reset options in Compose mode. These are workflow changes within the current animation environment, rather than a reason to treat Dreams as a painting-only app.

I would evaluate those changes through a small exercise: locate a drawing, compare it with its neighbours, return to the frame being edited, and adjust the movement around it. Those repeated actions occupy much of an animation session. A tool does not have to add a spectacular visual effect to make the process of working on a scene easier to understand.

Drawing and animation ask different questions

When the image itself is the main piece of work

With an illustration, I can spend a session on one relationship: how a shape reads against its background, how light separates a face from clothing, or whether an edge should be sharp or soft. I want the final image to make sense without a play button. That is why I would normally start a painting-led brief in Procreate.

Consider a hypothetical illustrated brand character. Before planning a walk or a wave, I would establish its silhouette, proportions, palette, and expression. If the character is difficult to recognise as a still image, movement introduces more decisions before the original ones are resolved. A clear design gives the animation something dependable to work with.

That does not mean every animation requires polished artwork at the beginning. Rough motion tests can reveal whether an idea works before detailed illustration begins. I would separate the questions: use an inexpensive sketch to test the action, then decide which drawings need finishing. The order follows the problem being investigated.

Procreate already has Animation Assist

The Animation Assist handbook describes onion skinning, playback, a visual timeline, and frame management. Onion skins let you compare a drawing with neighbouring drawings while you work. That makes the original app a reasonable place to explore a blink, a simple transformation, a bouncing shape, or another short drawn sequence.

For a beginner, a tiny loop is a useful exercise because it exposes timing without requiring a whole production. Draw a few positions, play them back, and notice where the action feels rushed or uncertain. Add a hold where the viewer needs time to read the pose. You can learn a great deal from improving one clear movement before adding a second character or a changing background.

I would move the project into a dedicated animation workspace when separate elements need independent timing, the sequence needs to be built around audio, or revisions increasingly involve rearranging moments. The limit is practical: is the current structure still helping you make the next change, or are you spending more effort working around it?

Three ways to think about motion in Dreams

Frame-by-frame animation asks you to design the changing drawing. Keyframing describes selected states over time and the movement between them. Performing records gestures as keyframes; the official Performing guide explains how those recordings appear beneath the content.

For a drawn flame changing shape, I would consider distinct drawings. For a finished symbol moving into position, I would consider keyframes. For exploring an expressive movement quickly, I might begin with a performance and assess its timing. Those are proposed approaches to different tasks, rather than a rule that one technique produces better work.

A useful test is whether the method leaves the right thing editable. If the client asks for a slower entrance, can you change the duration without repainting the whole sequence? If the character needs a different expression, can you revise the drawing without disturbing an unrelated background? Organisation becomes part of the creative result because it determines how confidently the work can evolve.

Example from my work: the Fuse logo animation

Fuse began as a lowercase wordmark with a soft, looping “s”. That curve already suggested movement in the static design. My animation follows the character of the letterforms instead of attaching an unrelated effect to them. The case study describes the result as a short brand introduction that can also work as a social ident or a bumper before video.

This is the distinction I want a comparison of the apps to make visible. Designing a curve and deciding when the viewer sees that curve are separate jobs. The first involves its weight, spacing, and relationship to neighbouring letters. The second involves direction, duration, rhythm, and how long the completed name remains readable.

When assessing a moving wordmark, I would watch it without sound first. Can I read the name? Does the action guide my eye through the letters, or keep pulling attention away from them? Does the final hold feel deliberate? A technically elaborate sequence can still be a poor introduction if the audience cannot identify the business at the end.

I would also check it at the size where it will actually appear. A subtle detail that looks attractive on an iPad may disappear inside a small website header or a compressed social video. The answer might be a simpler movement or a longer readable hold. Adding more animation is only useful when it improves what the audience receives.

For someone deciding between the apps, this project suggests a helpful exercise: design a simple wordmark, identify one feature that could motivate movement, and build a short introduction around that feature. Keep a still version beside the motion test. If the two feel like different identities, return to the design intention before expanding the sequence.

Example from my work: BPÉTALE identity and motion

BPÉTALE brings together Natural Essence, Travels & Tours, and Krunchies. The identity combines a flame-like, winged bird with a strong serif wordmark. That gives the design both an expressive symbol and a name that needs to remain legible. Its motion needs to respect those two roles.

My project page documents the work behind the identity as well as the moving result. For this comparison, it is important to distinguish a drawing time-lapse from an animated identity. A time-lapse shows the process of making artwork. An animation deliberately arranges what the audience sees through time. A recording of brushstrokes does not, on its own, demonstrate a character animation or a keyframed reveal.

The comparison becomes more useful when you ask what would happen during a revision. Imagine that the symbol needs to become larger for a particular placement while the name retains its size. If the artwork and motion have been organised appropriately, that request has a clear place in the project. If everything has been flattened early, a modest visual adjustment can become unnecessary reconstruction.

For a brand with several activities, I would also ask where a shared animated identity belongs. A website opening, a product presentation, and a travel clip may need different durations and framing. Reusing the same core artwork can maintain recognition while the delivery changes around it. That is a production decision to agree with the business, rather than a feature automatically supplied by either app.

This is why I think about the handover while making the work. A useful delivery contains a dependable still identity, the agreed moving versions, and a clear explanation of where each one should be used. The client should not have to extract a blurry logo from a video because the editable artwork or a suitable still export was forgotten.

A complete workflow from illustration to animation

The following is the workflow I would recommend for a small illustrated identity or short brand animation. It is an adaptable planning method, not a claim that every project in my portfolio followed these exact steps. The aim is to make the creative decisions and the delivery requirements support each other.

1. Define the result before opening a canvas

Write down where the piece will appear, what the viewer should understand, and which files are required. A square social post, a wide website banner, and a transparent overlay are different outputs. Decide whether audio matters and whether the work must loop. Ask who will approve the design and who will upload the finished files.

I would also ask what needs to be changeable later. Will the business update a tagline? Does the animation need another language? Will a future version use a different background? These questions determine which elements deserve separation before the drawing becomes detailed.

2. Plan dimensions and composition around the placement

Work from the destination rather than selecting the largest possible canvas by habit. Leave enough room for the movement and any crop that the placement requires. If several aspect ratios are needed, sketch them early. A composition that looks balanced in a wide frame may need a different arrangement in a vertical one.

For an illustrated subject, keep important features away from a crop that might remove them. For a wordmark, leave space for a readable final hold. These decisions are easier to address in a rough layout than after a detailed sequence has been completed.

3. Separate artwork according to what needs to move

Name elements by their role: symbol, wordmark, background, foreground texture, or accent. Keep an original source file before making delivery-specific changes. A layer should exist because it helps control the artwork or an anticipated revision, rather than because every brushstroke needs its own permanent layer.

For a hypothetical moving character, I would consider whether an arm or a background must move independently. For a logo, I would consider whether the mark and lettering have different entrance times. The structure should reflect the movement you intend to make.

4. Transfer a copy and inspect what arrives

The Dreams FAQ confirms that Procreate documents can be imported while retaining their layers. Keep the original artwork, import a working copy, and inspect the result before building a long sequence. Look at the edges, colours, grouping, and the elements you expect to animate.

Do not assume that a good-looking preview proves every later revision will be convenient. Try one representative change: move the symbol, adjust the timing, or replace a drawing. Discovering an awkward structure during that small test is much cheaper than discovering it after the animation has been approved.

5. Make a rough timing pass

Block out the beginning, the main action, and the final hold. Use unfinished shapes if necessary. Ask whether the sequence communicates its idea before you polish details. For a short identity animation, I would rather resolve when the name becomes readable than perfect a decorative accent that may later be removed.

Watch the sequence more than once, but also pause before replaying it. Repeated viewing makes you familiar with an action that a new viewer has only one chance to understand. Showing a rough version to someone unfamiliar with the idea can reveal where more time or a clearer transition is needed.

6. Refine motion and review the transitions

Look at spacing between positions, the speed around changes of direction, and the relationship between moving elements. Decide where stillness helps the composition. A loop deserves special attention at the join: the last moment should lead convincingly back to the first, unless a visible reset is an intentional part of the idea.

Keep revisions focused. If a movement feels uncertain, change one relationship and watch again. Changing the shape, speed, colour, and framing together makes it difficult to understand which adjustment helped. Animation improves through observation as much as through adding more detail.

7. Export, test, and package the handover

Open the exported file outside the creation app. Check its first and last frames, crop, background, audio if present, and behaviour in the intended placement. A source file playing correctly is only one part of delivery. The actual published asset is what the audience will experience.

Package the agreed source files and exports with useful names and a short usage note. Keep a backup outside the active working copy. My guide to animating a logo in Procreate Dreams gives another view of planning a compact identity animation.

Hardware, exports, and the part after the canvas

Check the actual iPad, rather than a headline capability

The current official FAQs specify iPadOS 16.3 or newer for the versions covered here. Compatibility is the starting point. The Dreams compatibility table also differentiates devices by content tracks, video tracks, supported resolution, and ProRes support. Procreate's maximum canvas size similarly depends on the device.

I would not choose hardware from a single impressive resolution figure. Think about the kind of scene you intend to build and the files you must deliver. A small logo loop and a long sequence with several imported videos make different demands. Check the specific device entry and leave room for the actual project, its source material, and backups.

Keep source files separate from viewing files

The Procreate file-types guide explains its native .procreate format and its image import/export options. The Dreams export guide distinguishes editable .drm movies from rendered outputs. An exported video is useful for viewing; it is not a substitute for a project someone may need to edit.

For a handover, I would make that distinction explicit. Give the client the version they can upload today, and agree separately whether editable source is part of the delivery. A folder containing several unexplained files leaves the next person guessing. A short note can say which version belongs on the website and which one is retained for future revisions.

Match the export to the destination

The current Dreams Advanced Export documentation covers video, GIF, current-frame, and frame-sequence outputs. It documents H.264, HEVC, and ProRes video options; transparency uses alpha-capable options such as HEVC or ProRes 4444. Available capabilities and the intended playback environment still matter.

For web delivery, I would check the actual browsers and devices the site serves. A high-quality master and a lightweight viewing copy can have different purposes. The MP4 and WebM files embedded with my work are website delivery assets; their presence here is not a claim that Dreams directly exports WebM.

A GIF is convenient in some placements, but it is not automatically the best choice for every animation. Inspect file size, colour treatment, and visual quality alongside compatibility. For a website, retain a useful poster image and let the surrounding page remain understandable before the video starts. Motion should support the work without making the page depend on playback.

Agree ownership, licences, and maintenance

Keep track of any third-party fonts, music, and other assets used in a commission. Check the licence for the intended use rather than assuming that an asset being available inside an app makes every commercial use acceptable. This is particularly relevant when a personal experiment becomes a client's published identity.

I also want the business to know where the files live and who can access them. An animation is easier to maintain when another authorised person can identify the approved version and find its source. That small amount of organisation protects the value of the creative work after the initial launch.

Which one should you buy or learn first?

If your main goal is illustration

I would begin with Procreate and a small series of finished images. Learn to control shape, value, colour, edges, and composition. Keep the brief narrow enough to finish. Buying an additional animation app will not decide the visual direction of an unfinished painting for you.

Once you have a piece you want to move, try a simple Animation Assist exercise. That gives you a specific reason to evaluate Dreams later: you will know which part of the animation process you want to make more flexible.

If your main goal is animation

Starting directly in Dreams can be sensible. You do not need to purchase Procreate first. Begin with simple drawings or suitable existing assets and concentrate on timing, readable poses, and transitions. You can learn visual fundamentals alongside animation rather than treating proficiency in a separate app as an entry requirement.

I would keep the first project small: one action, a clear beginning and end, and a manageable number of elements. Finishing a short sequence teaches the whole process, including export and review. A much larger unfinished project can conceal which skills actually need attention.

If you create identities and client content

Both apps may be useful when a project includes original artwork and a moving presentation. I would still identify the outputs before deciding that both are necessary. A client requesting a static mark needs a dependable identity and suitable files. A client requesting motion needs an agreed sequence and delivery format as well.

Both apps are sold separately as one-time purchases according to the official FAQ. Check the current regional App Store listings for the amount you will actually pay. Budget for the whole working setup, including a compatible device, storage, and any licensed materials the project needs.

Frequently asked questions

Is Procreate Dreams a replacement for Procreate?

I would treat them as overlapping tools with different priorities. Choose around the work you need to complete. A painter may finish without Dreams; an animator may work directly in Dreams; an illustrated motion project may benefit from using both.

Can I animate without buying Dreams?

Yes. Procreate includes Animation Assist. It is a useful place to test short drawn sequences and learn timing. Consider Dreams when the project benefits from its animation workspace and the way it organises changes over time.

Can I draw inside Dreams?

Yes. Drawing is part of its animation workflow, including the revised drawing tools documented for Dreams 2. Whether you want a separate painting app depends on how much of your work involves developing and finishing still artwork.

Do I need to learn drawing before animation?

You can begin with simple shapes and learn both together. For character work, drawing practice helps you communicate form and expression. For a moving wordmark or assembled graphic, timing and composition can be productive starting points even without detailed character illustration.

Will either app make a logo into a complete brand identity?

The tool can help create assets, but the identity still requires decisions about recognition, typography, colour, application, and consistency. I would agree the scope and deliverables with the business. A moving introduction is one useful expression of an identity, rather than the entire identity system.

Are the examples here tests of the latest versions?

No. Fuse and BPÉTALE are examples of my published work. They help explain design and motion decisions. The version and feature comparison is based on official documentation checked for this update; I am not presenting measured performance tests or claiming those projects were rebuilt for this article.

What would I practise next?

Take a simple mark or illustration and make two deliveries: a clear still image and a short moving version. Explain why the movement belongs to that design. Then test both in a real placement. The exercise connects drawing, motion, and delivery more usefully than collecting a long list of effects.
